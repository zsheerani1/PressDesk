Chapter One

The clock in the harbour office ran on the tide, not on the hours. Ada Mercer had learned that before she learned to read. When the water was high the brass hand pointed straight up, and when the boats sat tilted in the mud it hung down like a tired arm.

Her father had wound it every morning for thirty-one years. Now he was gone, and the key sat in Ada's coat pocket, heavier than it had any right to be.

"You'll keep it going," said Mrs Pell from the bakery, not as a question.

Ada nodded, because nodding was easier than explaining that she had a train ticket to Leeds folded inside the same pocket.

CHAPTER 2: The Harbour Master

The new harbour master arrived on a Tuesday, in a car far too clean for the coast road. His name was Elliot Shaw and he wore a tie, which nobody in Penmarrow had done since the last funeral.

He stood in front of the tide clock for a long time.

"It's eleven minutes slow," he said at last.

"It isn't slow," Ada told him. "The moon is early."

He wrote that down in a small black notebook, and she could not decide whether he was making fun of her or taking her seriously. Later she would realise he did not know the difference himself.

Chapter 3 – Low Water

At the spring low the sea pulled back further than anyone could remember. Children ran out across the sand to the old wreck, the one that only showed its ribs twice a year. Ada went with them, boots in her hand.

Inside the wreck, wedged between two blackened timbers, she found a tin box. It was rusted shut. Someone had scratched initials into the lid: T.M.

Her grandfather had been Thomas Mercer.

She carried it back before the water turned, and she did not tell Elliot Shaw, or Mrs Pell, or anyone at all.

Chapter IV

The box held a second key, smaller than the first, and a letter that began, "If the clock ever stops, look behind the face."

Ada read it twice in the harbour office with the door locked. Outside, the tide was rising and the brass hand was climbing towards noon.

She took the train ticket out of her pocket and put it on the desk. Then she took it back again. Then she fetched the stepladder.

Behind the face of the tide clock, where no one had looked in fifty years, there was a small door with a keyhole exactly the size of the key in her hand.
